# Clarity Checklist for Doctoral Writing

A systematic checklist to evaluate and enhance clarity, conciseness, and academic rigor in scholarly writing. Applicable to academic manuscripts across the humanities, social sciences, and natural sciences.

> **Role**: Verification checklist (VERIFY). For governing principles, see `SKILL.md`; for systematic issue typology, see `common-issues.md`; for practical transformation patterns, see `academic-quick-reference.md`; for extended dissertation case studies, see `before-after-examples.md`.

## 1. Sentence-Level Clarity

### Subject-Verb Relationship

- [ ] **Subject is clearly identifiable**
  - Can you identify the grammatical subject of each sentence within 3 seconds?
  - Is the subject positioned near the opening of the sentence?
  - Does the grammatical subject represent the real conceptual agent?

- [ ] **Subject and verb are close together**
  - Are there fewer than 7–8 words between the subject and the main verb?
  - Have long intervening clauses or parenthetical modifiers been repositioned or streamlined?
  - Does the core predicate follow immediately after the subject?

- [ ] **Verb is strong and precise**
  - Have weak verbs (is, has, does, make) been replaced with precise analytical verbs?
  - Are core actions expressed directly as verbs rather than buried inside nominalizations?

### Sentence Structure

- [ ] **Sentence length is appropriate**
  - Three-tier sentence length guidelines (identical to `SKILL.md`):
    - **Recommended**: 15–25 words
    - **Caution**: Exceeding 25 words warrants evaluation for splitting
    - **Maximum**: 30 words — mandatory division into multiple sentences
  - Can compound thoughts be split into two discrete, logically linked sentences?

- [ ] **One main idea per sentence**
  - Does each sentence focus on a single claim, finding, or logical proposition?
  - Have compound ideas been separated into distinct sequential units?

- [ ] **Parallel structure for related ideas**
  - Do listed items, coordinate clauses, and correlative pairs follow identical grammatical patterns?
  - Are comparative clauses balanced symmetrically in syntax and emphasis?

### Voice and Tense

- [ ] **Active voice used appropriately**
  - Is active voice used when describing the researcher's analytical decisions and core findings?
  - Is passive voice reserved strictly for instances where the agent is unknown, immaterial, or dictated by disciplinary norms (e.g., laboratory procedures)?

- [ ] **Consistent and appropriate tense**
  - Are completed experimental procedures and past study findings reported in the past tense?
  - Are established general truths, theoretical principles, and ongoing debates presented in the present tense?

## 2. Word-Level Precision

### Terminology

- [ ] **Technical terms defined on first use**
  - Is an explicit conceptual definition provided upon first introducing specialized terminology?
  - Is the definition clear, rigorous, and accessible to the target scholarly audience?

- [ ] **Acronyms spelled out**
  - Are all acronyms expanded in full upon first appearance followed by the abbreviation in parentheses?
  - Format: "Structural Equation Modeling (SEM)" → subsequently "SEM"

- [ ] **Consistent terminology — Terminology SOT**
  - Is the exact same term used for identical constructs throughout the entire manuscript?
  - Has "elegant variation" (arbitrary synonym substitution for technical constructs) been eliminated?
  - Does terminology remain identical from first introduction to conclusion? (Absolute Criterion 2)
  - If terminology consistency conflicts with newly established scholarly precision, is precision prioritized and the SOT retroactively updated? (Absolute Criterion 1 > 2)

### Word Choice

- [ ] **Precise and specific words**
  - Have vague descriptors been replaced with concrete terms or quantified metrics?
  - Do chosen terms convey exact scholarly distinctions without ambiguity?

- [ ] **Unnecessary modifiers removed**
  - Have empty intensifiers ("very", "quite", "substantially", "rather") been purged?
  - Are remaining qualifiers strictly essential to empirical meaning?

- [ ] **Jargon minimized**
  - Is specialized terminology restricted to necessary theoretical constructs?
  - Can obscure jargon be replaced with transparent academic phrasing without sacrificing rigor?

## 3. Paragraph-Level Coherence

### Paragraph Structure

- [ ] **Clear topic sentence**
  - Does the opening sentence articulate the central claim or analytical focus of the paragraph?
  - Can the reader accurately predict the development of the paragraph from the topic sentence?

- [ ] **One main idea per paragraph**
  - Is each paragraph dedicated to developing a single core theme?
  - Have overextended paragraphs addressing multiple topics been decomposed into separate units?

- [ ] **Supporting sentences develop topic**
  - Does every supporting sentence directly substantiate, illustrate, or qualify the topic sentence?
  - Is there a progressive, logical accumulation of evidence?

### Transitions and Flow

- [ ] **Effective transitions between sentences**
  - Are logical relationships between consecutive sentences explicitly signaled?
  - Are transitional adverbs (consequently, furthermore, conversely) selected with conceptual precision?

- [ ] **Clear connections between paragraphs**
  - Does each paragraph bridge logically to preceding and subsequent paragraphs?
  - Are major thematic shifts prefaced with clear transitional signposts?

- [ ] **Logical argument progression**
  - Do premises cumulatively build toward the central dissertation thesis?
  - Is the argumentative trajectory robust against counterarguments?

## 4. Conciseness

### Wordiness Reduction

- [ ] **Redundancies eliminated**
  - Have pleonastic phrases ("past history", "final outcome", "future projections", "advance warning") been removed?
  - Has tautological phrasing been stripped across all sections?

- [ ] **Wordy phrases replaced**
  - "in order to" → "to"
  - "due to the fact that" → "because"
  - "at this point in time" → "currently" or "now"
  - "in the event that" → "if"
  - "with regard to" → "regarding" or "about"
  - "it is important to note that" → deleted or stated directly

- [ ] **Excessive prepositional strings reduced**
  - Have chains of prepositional phrases (three or more consecutive "of", "in", "by") been consolidated using possessives or direct compound nouns?

### Nominalization

- [ ] **Unnecessary nominalizations converted**
  - "conduct an investigation of" → "investigate"
  - "reach a decision regarding" → "decide"
  - "provide an explanation of" → "explain"
  - "perform an analysis on" → "analyze"

### Constructions to Avoid

- [ ] **Expletive constructions ("There is/are", "It is...") minimized**
  - "There are three factors that influence..." → "Three factors influence..."
  - "It is essential that researchers examine..." → "Researchers must examine..."

- [ ] **Hedging reduced to calibrated levels**
  - Are epistemic markers ("perhaps", "possibly", "might seem to") used only where warranted by empirical uncertainty?
  - Has habitual, apologetic hedging been eliminated in favor of definitive scholarly assertions?

## 5. Academic Rigor

### Citations and Evidence

- [ ] **Claims supported with evidence**
  - Is every theoretical claim, empirical generalization, or factual assertion substantiated with citations or data?
  - Are cited sources authoritative, peer-reviewed, and up to date?

- [ ] **Citations properly formatted — Citation SOT**
  - Does citation syntax adhere strictly to the required style guide (APA 7th, Chicago, MLA, etc.)?
  - Does every in-text citation match an entry in the reference list, and vice versa?
  - Is a single citation format maintained consistently throughout the entire manuscript? (Absolute Criterion 2)

### Scholarly Tone

- [ ] **Appropriate formality maintained**
  - Is a formal, dignified scholarly register maintained throughout all chapters?
  - Have contractions, colloquial expressions, and rhetorical flourishes been purged?

- [ ] **Objective language used**
  - Are findings reported with analytical detachment, free from emotional or polemical language?
  - Have subjective value judgments been replaced with empirical characterizations?

- [ ] **Discipline-appropriate voice and person — Style SOT**
  - Is authorial person (first vs. third person) aligned with disciplinary standards?
  - Is authorial voice consistent throughout the entire manuscript? (Absolute Criterion 2)
  - If consistency would undermine argumentative depth in a specific reflective or qualitative section, has an explicit, reasoned exception been documented? (Absolute Criterion 1 > 2)

## 6. Discipline-Specific Conventions

- [ ] **Required style guide followed**
  - Are institutional or target journal formatting standards (APA, Chicago, MLA, CSE) executed without discrepancy?
  - Are layout, typography, and heading hierarchies applied uniformly?

- [ ] **Disciplinary norms respected**
  - Are disciplinary expectations regarding voice, chapter structure, and empirical reporting observed?

## How to Use This Checklist

### Full Manuscript Review
1. Apply the checklist to 2–3 representative paragraphs in each major chapter.
2. Identify systematic error patterns (e.g., pervasive passive voice or prepositional stacking).
3. Execute systematic, manuscript-wide revisions targeting identified patterns.
4. Re-evaluate high-priority chapters post-revision.

### Focused Revision
1. Diagnose a specific weakness (e.g., excessive nominalization, weak topic sentences).
2. Isolate the corresponding checklist section.
3. Apply targeted surgical edits across the affected text.
4. Verify improvements against the checklist criteria.

### Self-Editing Workflow
1. Allow 24–48 hours to elapse between initial draft completion and revision.
2. Read the draft aloud to detect awkward phrasing, rhythmic breaks, and run-on sentences.
3. Apply checklist items systematically from macro to micro tiers.
4. Iterate until all criteria pass.

### Priority Tiers

**High Priority** (governs fundamental clarity and scholarly validity):
- Subject-verb alignment and physical proximity
- Explicit definition of specialized terminology
- One core proposition per sentence and per paragraph
- Empirical grounding for all analytical claims

**Medium Priority** (governs readability and prose efficiency):
- Adherence to word-count boundaries (15–25 words)
- Balanced voice distribution (active vs. passive)
- Precision in transitional signaling
- Eradication of pleonasms and wordy stock phrases

**Low Priority** (governs surface polish):
- Uniform formatting conventions
- Fine-grained stylistic variance
- Granular calibration of elective hedges
